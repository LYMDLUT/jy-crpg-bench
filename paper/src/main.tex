\begin{abstract}
Long-horizon agency requires a model to perceive an unfamiliar world, decide
for itself what is worth doing, and hold one plan across hours of dependent
decisions. We present \bench{}, a benchmark that places a vision-language
model inside \game{金庸群俠傳} (\emph{Heroes of Jin Yong}, Heluo Studio
1996), an unmodified commercial open-world role-playing game under DOS
emulation, and scores it against the ending the game defines, which requires
recovering the fourteen books of the Jin Yong canon and takes a first-time
human player tens of hours. The environment lies far outside the training distribution of
current vision encoders, with a $320\times200$ framebuffer, a $45^\circ$
isometric projection in which no key moves straight up the screen, Traditional
Chinese rendered as 16-pixel bitmap glyphs, and objectives stated only inside
its fiction. Every model plays through the same brief and control surface,
with the frame as its observation and keys as its actions, and with no memory
scaffold and no access to game state, so a run measures the model. Progress is read
from the memory of the emulated machine and from the save the game writes for
itself, so every milestone is an event that pixel similarity cannot
manufacture, and one instrument spans the whole distance: a ladder of
machine-verified milestones while models learn to move, and completion time
once they finish. We report \NmodelsDistinct{} frontier models against a
seeded random baseline under a \NbudgetMin{}-minute budget, and release \bench{} as a live, replayable
public arena at \url{https://github.com/hanxiao/jy-crpg-bench}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Frontier models prove competition mathematics and write working compilers,
yet the tasks they complete reliably are still measured in hours of human
work \citep{kwa2025horizon}. Long-horizon evaluation takes two forms. Episodic benchmarks set a goal and score its
completion; continuing benchmarks give the model an environment
with no terminal state and score what accumulates
\citep{backlund2025vending,fan2026ecommerce}. Open-world games belong to the
second family and add a requirement the business simulations do not: the
model has to recover its own state from raw perception, because nothing in
the environment reports it.

bag (g), the outdoor menu with the party list it opens (h), and a turn of
battle with the menu and record of the acting character (i).}
\label{fig:env}
\end{figure}

Existing game benchmarks each remove one part of that problem to isolate
another. BALROG evaluates six research environments in language-only and
language-vision modes \citep{paglieri2025balrog}; TextQuests removes
perception entirely \citep{phan2025textquests}; ARC-AGI-3 excludes language
and cultural priors by construction \citep{arc2026arcagi3}; VideoGameBench
detects progress in real-time action games by matching against checkpoint
images taken from walkthroughs \citep{zhang2025videogamebench}; MineExplorer
filters out tasks whose solutions depend on game-specific knowledge
\citep{ju2026mineexplorer}. Each removal is principled, and each leaves the
combination untested. We keep the parts together, because acting through
unfamiliar perception, unfamiliar language and unfamiliar culture at once is
what a deployed model is asked to do.

\bench{} runs a single deep environment, following the precedent of Crafter
and NetHack \citep{hafner2021benchmarking,kuttler2020nethack}. The environment is
\game{金庸群俠傳}, a 1996 Chinese role-playing game that remains a landmark
of its genre, executed as its original DOS binary under emulation with no
modification. The game presents an open world built from the fourteen novels
of Jin Yong: a two-tier map, several hundred named characters each carrying
level, experience, skills, items and reputation, and an ending that requires
recovering fourteen books scattered across the world. A first human
playthrough takes tens of hours. The model receives the framebuffer and a key
vocabulary through one fixed, minimal harness, and nothing else, so what
varies between runs is the model.

We measure against that ending. The fourteen books sit behind items, factions
and martial arts that have to be acquired in order, in a world that reports
nothing about itself, and the game records whether they have been recovered.
A model that finished would have held one plan across tens of hours of
self-directed play, which is the capability long-horizon evaluation exists to
detect. The instrument therefore covers the whole distance: a ladder of
machine-verified milestones orders the field while models are learning to
move through the world, and completion time orders it once they can finish,
so the benchmark gains a speedrun ranking at the point where a pass-or-fail
score would saturate.

Three properties make the environment adversarial for current models, and
\cref{fig:env} shows the world they apply to. Perception is out of
distribution on several axes at once. Downsampling costs vision-language
models accuracy \citep{niu2025resolution}, degraded input costs them more
\citep{usama2025corruptions}, out-of-distribution images degrade recognition
even of familiar categories \citep{lin2026oodbench}, and text rendered as
pixels is understood less well than the same text supplied as tokens
\citep{liu2026vistabench}, and precise spatial reading fails once primitives
are small or adjacent \citep{rahmanzadehgervi2024blind,wang2024spatialeval};
a $320\times200$
frame of 1996 bitmap art carrying 16-pixel Traditional Chinese glyphs sits at
the hard end of all five. Control
is projected. The world is drawn in a $45^\circ$ isometric projection, so the
four movement keys run along screen diagonals, and a model that reasons in
screen coordinates walks in circles. Progress is self-directed. The game
keeps no quest log, draws no objective marker and reports no score. The brief
supplies the opening route, from the first room to the compass, and a manual
of the controls; from the compass on, deciding where to go and what is worth
doing is part of the problem. ARC-AGI-3 places the same
goal-setting requirement at the centre of agency \citep{arc2026arcagi3}.

Measuring such an environment from the screen is unreliable, and the field
has begun to say so: GameWorld names heuristic verification as the obstacle
to standardised game-agent evaluation and pairs each of its tasks with a
state-verifiable metric \citep{ouyang2026gameworld}. We take the same
position and push it into the emulator. The benchmark reads progress out of
the memory of the running machine, from the character array the game
maintains, and out of the save file the game writes when asked, which is
where the party and the world position are true. The model has no call that
returns any of this, and nothing read from the screen enters a score.

We make three contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
\item We present \bench{}, a long-horizon benchmark on an unmodified
commercial open-world role-playing game whose difficulty comes from named and
independently checkable properties of the environment: out-of-distribution
low-resolution perception, isometric control, Traditional Chinese grounding,
a keyboard action space of which a small subset does anything, and
persistent character state that only coherent play advances.

\item We score progress in the terms the game keeps. Character records, the
party and the fourteen books are read from emulator memory and from a save
the game performs for itself, validated against the archives the release
ships, and measurement never writes to the machine it observes. Every rung of
the ladder is an event the model has to cause, and the terminal rung is the
ending the game defines.

\item We evaluate \NmodelsDistinct{} frontier models under the same harness
and against a seeded random baseline under a wall-clock budget, and release
the benchmark, its harness and every recorded run as a public, replayable
arena.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Game Environments as Agent Benchmarks}

Games have been the standard setting for sequential decision-making since the
Arcade Learning Environment \citep{bellemare2013arcade}, and language and
vision-language agents have produced a second generation of game benchmarks.
BALROG aggregates six reinforcement-learning environments under one protocol
and reports a persistent gap between what a model knows about a game and what
it does inside one \citep{paglieri2025balrog}. lmgame-Bench isolates how much
of a game result belongs to the harness \citep{hu2026lmgame}, and Orak spans
twelve popular titles with configurable agentic modules \citep{park2026orak}.
VideoGameBench is closest to our setting, running real 1990s games from raw
pixels under a no-scaffold rule \citep{zhang2025videogamebench}. GameWorld
standardises the action interface across 34 browser games and pairs every
task with a state-verifiable metric \citep{ouyang2026gameworld}. MineExplorer
evaluates open-world exploration in Minecraft and finds that models handle
single-hop tasks but degrade once hidden prerequisites must be coordinated
over a longer trajectory \citep{ju2026mineexplorer}, which is the structure
of the opening sequence in our environment as well. The closest precedents
to our setting are the public Pok\'emon runs. Claude
3.7 Sonnet reached three gym badges from screen pixels behind a memory
scaffold, where an earlier model had not left the first house
\citep{anthropic2025pokemon}; Gemini 2.5 Pro finished Pok\'emon Blue in 813
hours behind a harness that translated the RAM of the game into text, and an
ablation without vision performed about as well \citep{comanici2025gemini};
the PokeAgent Challenge turned the setting into a speedrunning track scored
by completion time over milestones \citep{karten2026pokeagent}. Crafter
established the single-environment, achievement-based design we follow
\citep{hafner2021benchmarking}, and NetHack remains the depth extreme for
learned agents \citep{kuttler2020nethack}.

\bench{} differs from these in running one persistent open world with
Traditional Chinese as its only text, in verifying progress from emulator
memory and from the save file of the game, and in scoring against the
completion condition the game defines, which gives the leaderboard a second
scale in completion time.

\subsection{Long-Horizon Evaluation and Its Measurement}

Vending-Bench established long-term coherence as the property that separates
long-horizon tasks from long chains of short ones
\citep{backlund2025vending}, and E-Commerce Bench extends the continuing
formulation to a year of deterministic business operation
\citep{fan2026ecommerce}. Both keep perception out of the loop. TextQuests
shares our self-directed, long-horizon framing while removing vision
\citep{phan2025textquests}, TextAtari stretches horizons to $10^5$ steps over
text renderings \citep{li2025textatari}, ARC-AGI-3 isolates fluid agency by
excluding language and culture \citep{arc2026arcagi3}, and AutumnBench probes
world-model quality through environment-level queries
\citep{warrier2025benchmarking}. UltraHorizon makes exploration under hidden
rules the task itself \citep{luo2025ultrahorizon}, and \citet{kapoor2026openworld}
observe that benchmark design has favoured tasks that are precisely
specified, automatically graded and short, and call for open-world
evaluation. Navigation from rendered images is a documented weakness: on
mazes, one-shot navigation collapses for every model by ten cells a side
\citep{jiang2026lostaggregation}, and the failures are attributed to looping
\citep{einarsson2025mazeeval}.

Measurement is where long horizons strain evaluation. The ABC checklist
catalogues task-validity and outcome-validity failures across agentic
benchmarks \citep{zhu2025establishing}, an audit of agent traces finds
reward hacking in a majority of them on two suites \citep{shao2026validity},
and a survey of the horizon gap finds that the field answers uninformative
outcome signals by building denser step-level ones
\citep{chen2026horizongap}. State-based verification has precedent outside
games, where $\tau$-bench compares the final database state with an
annotated goal \citep{yao2024taubench}. Our milestone ladder is an instance
of the same answer, read from machine state so that dense signals cannot be
mistaken for progress.

\section{\bench{}}
\label{sec:bench}

\subsection{Environment}
\label{sec:env}

\game{金庸群俠傳} (Heluo Studio, 1996) opens with the protagonist in a small
compound, and the fiction supplies the only standing instruction: enter the
world of the Jin Yong novels, join its factions, learn its martial arts, and
recover the fourteen books that end the game. The world is two-tier, with
many local scenes such as buildings, sects and caves attached to one outdoor
map of $480\times480$ tiles \citep{scarsty2026kyscpp}. For each of \Mrecords{} named characters the game maintains a
record carrying level, experience, health, stamina, learned skills, carried
items, reputation and aptitude. Progression is nonlinear and gated. Every
scene carries an entry condition, and at the start all of them are closed
except the home, five inns and the house of the hermit \game{南賢}; events
open the rest, and the opening sets their order. The compound holds a
searchable chest, one speaking character and one exit tile. Beyond it, the
path south leads to the hermit, whose long conversation opens the house of
the first companion, and a cabinet beside him holds the compass, the only
source of coordinates the game offers. The companion joins after a yes-or-no
prompt at which the confirm key means no. Fights are turn-based on the scene
grid. Each turn a character moves along the four axes and then strikes, uses
an item, waits or rests. Skills draw on inner force, and the first scripted
fight opens by draining it. An automatic mode hands the character to the AI
of the game, which alternates striking and resting and loses to an opponent
who heals. A party that wanders elsewhere first meets storylines whose fights
it cannot survive at level one, and a lost fight ends the game, which then
offers nothing but its own load menu.

\Cref{fig:env} shows the world at the resolution the model receives it. Every
objective, prompt and statistic reaches the model as Traditional Chinese
rendered at sixteen pixels a line, and the character sheet it would have to
read is the same record the benchmark reads from memory. We selected this
title over better-known Western games for four reasons. Its text is
Traditional Chinese throughout, a script that multimodal evaluation has
largely passed over \citep{tam2025vistw}, so cross-lingual grounding is a
first-class obstacle. It is menu-driven, so a model that deliberates between actions is
not structurally disadvantaged as it would be in a real-time title
\citep[cf.][]{zhang2025videogamebench}. Its progression state is rich enough
to measure at the level of individual character statistics. And its
$45^\circ$ isometric projection makes the mapping from screen to action
something the model must infer.

\subsection{Observation and Action Space}
\label{sec:interface}

The model observes the raw framebuffer at $320\times200$ and acts by pressing
keys. A scored session serves the native frame and nothing else, so no
information is added or smoothed away, and nothing is annotated: there is no
set-of-marks overlay and no accessibility tree
\citep[cf.][]{xie2024osworld}. The action space is the DOS keyboard,
\MkeyNames{} names over \MkeyCodes{} distinct keys, and \cref{fig:keymap}
shows how few of them the game answers. Movement runs along the four numpad
diagonals, with the arrow keys aliased onto the same axes, so a model that
presses \texttt{up} expecting to walk up the screen moves up and to the right.
Perception has traps of its own. The view follows the character except near
the edge of a scene, where the background holds still and the sprite walks
across it, so a model that reads its movement from the scrolling of the
background loses count of the tiles it walked there. Canopies and roofs are
drawn over the character, and the entrance of a building is a single tile on
one face of its wall, so reaching a door means walking around the compound to
the side the door is on.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.94\linewidth]{figures/keymap.pdf}
\caption{The action space. The API accepts \MkeyNames{} key names over
\MkeyCodes{} distinct keys; outside dialogue the game answers the nine picked
out here, any key advances a dialogue, and a model has to find them.}
\label{fig:keymap}
\end{figure}

There is one action. It presses a key, or a list of keys in order, holding
each for a requested number of emulated frames, ten by default, and returns
once the screen has reacted and held still, waiting through a scene
transition if one starts, so the frame a model receives is the one to act on.
A repeat is a list of the same key and a menu path is a list, and there is no
call that lets the game run without input, because the game only moves on
input and the action already waits for the result. The hold is counted in
frames because the game samples its keyboard once per game-loop iteration and
a shorter pulse can be consumed without registering. A scored session answers
four HTTP calls, listed in \cref{app:brief}: the screen, the brief, the key
names and the action. The development runner adds three snapshot calls that a
scored session answers with 404, since a run with an out-of-band rewind is not
a run.
The save and load menu of the game stays open to the model, as to a human
player, so a model that loses a fight can reload the last save the game
wrote, a rewind within the run that the recording shows.

\subsection{Harness and Session Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

The benchmark supplies the harness, and the harness is minimal. The brief is
the whole instruction the model receives: the calls that constitute play, the
rules of a run, the opening route as far as the compass, and a manual of the
controls and systems of the game, served in English and Chinese with the on-screen Traditional
terms of the game kept verbatim, so the language a model reasons in is not
itself a handicap. The model reaches the game through two tools, which look
at the screen and press one or more keys, and an action returns only its
metadata, so the model looks when it decides to. There is no memory scaffold, no map, no pathfinder and no access to the
state of the game. This separates the setting from the public Pok\'emon
runs, where the harness translated RAM into text and did the perception
\citep{comanici2025gemini}, and it is what makes the leaderboard a ranking of
models: harness choice moves game results
\citep{hu2026lmgame,karten2026harness}, and on long-horizon tasks the
variance a harness induces can exceed the variance between models
\citep{zhang2026harness}. A session is created under a declared name; the
reference harness requires a thinking level, one of seven from off to max, at
launch, and files model and level with the usage report of
\cref{sec:metrics}.

A session is created with a playtime, \DefaultBudgetMin{} minutes by default
and up to twenty-four hours, and the clock starts at the first playable
moment. Each
session is a separate emulator process with a private copy of the game,
which every host supplies itself as described in \cref{app:licence}, with
its save slots reset to a new game, so no run inherits the progress of
another. A session ends only when its budget lapses or it completes; a long
pause between keys is the model's to spend, and there is no inactivity
cutoff.
When the budget lapses the session renders its recording
to video, computes its metrics, and appends itself to the public catalogue.
\Cref{fig:apparatus} shows the apparatus, and \cref{app:infra} lists the
session parameters.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/architecture.pdf}
\caption{The apparatus. One process per session loads the game core
in-process and answers the control API; the recording feeds the public

\end{figure}

Because the control surface is plain HTTP, the harness is small and is
released with the benchmark, and any client can drive a session for
development or spectating. Every run publishes a time-compressed video and
a keypress timeline, so the behaviour behind a score can be reviewed.

\subsection{State-Verifiable Progress}
\label{sec:measure}

The game keeps its persistent state in one archive: a base block with the
world position, the party roster and the shared bag, followed by
\Mrecords{} character records of \MrecordBytes{} bytes and an item table. We
validated that layout against the opening machine image and the archives the
release ships, where only \Moverflow{} of \Mrecords{} records violate
$\mathrm{hp}\le\mathrm{maxHp} \wedge \mathrm{mp}\le\mathrm{maxMp}$, and located
the same array in the live emulated machine by anchoring on one character
name and confirming the two neighbouring records at the correct stride, since
the name recurs in the image and the layout moves between runs. The bag in front of that array is the working copy and moves
the moment an item is taken, so the item count and the books held are
current. Reading is passive: the benchmark serialises the machine but never
loads state into it. The one thing it presses for itself is the save
described next, which costs the game a few seconds of menu time outside the
accounting of the run and leaves the screen as it found it.

For the party roster and the world position the benchmark reads the game's
own save, the archive it writes in the format already validated against the
shipped ones. The game offers its save menu on the world map alone and
announces this through the height of the menu, so an attempt opens the menu,
counts its rows and withdraws where saving is not offered, leaving the screen
unchanged; a save that lands is itself the evidence that the party has crossed
onto the world map. The archive the game writes is decoded like the shipped
ones, and neither it nor anything read from it is reachable through the
control surface.

Progress is summarised as eight milestones read from this ground truth, in
the spirit of the achievements of Crafter \citep{hafner2021benchmarking}, and
\cref{tab:ladder} lists them with the evidence each rests on. Only the first
concerns the harness; the rest are the numbers the game keeps about itself.
The rungs fall into two horizons that the game itself draws. The opening
needs no fight: the chest in the first room, the exit tile, the compass in
the cabinet of the hermit, and the companion whose house that conversation
opens and who joins through dialogue are reached by walking, reading and
answering. The campaign begins with the first fight, which is what experience
and levels require, and runs through the chains of items, factions and fights
that the books sit behind. The default budget is sized to the opening and the
extended budgets to the campaign. The rungs are listed in the order the
opening imposes, but a run is credited with every rung it reaches, so the
count does not depend on the path taken, and a rung whose instrumentation
post-dates a run is reported as unmeasured, since an unmeasured zero and a
true zero are different claims.

The ladder points at the ending of the game. The fourteen books are what the
game requires before its final sequence can be played, and the archive
records every book held, so the count of books carries the ladder from its
last rung to the threshold of that sequence, and the fourteenth book is the
completion event the benchmark times: the first read that finds all fourteen
held latches the played time. Sessions run to twenty-four hours on the same
protocol, and a session that completes carries that time, which orders a
field in which every entrant passes. One instrument therefore
covers both horizons, and the benchmark keeps measuring after the first model
finishes.

That ladder is the frozen score of the short benchmark and it stays fixed. For
the multi-hour budgets the protocol allows, the release adds a second,
additive report over the same ground truth: a finer trajectory that records
the scenes a run reached, the battles it survived, the skills it learned and
the books it gathered, out to the ending, with the walkthrough's hard
prerequisites as dependency gates between them. Each component is scored
against a fixed denominator with its coverage reported beside it, so a run
instrumented on only part of the trajectory cannot post a falsely high number,
and a milestone a run never measured is marked unmeasured rather than counted
as failed. The report never rescores the short ladder and never feeds back
into the short field's intervals; it exists so that a long run holding no book
still leaves an ordered record of how far its plan carried. Its checkpoint
contract and component weights are released with the benchmark.

\subsection{Effort}
\label{sec:metrics}

The ladder is the score, and nothing read from the screen enters it. Beside
it the session records effort. The wall clock of every decision call is kept,
so the played time and the think time between calls are measured on the
server for any client. The tokens a run spends are a property of the client,
so the reference harness meters them: after a run it sums the usage the
provider reported for every assistant turn on the active branch of its
session, as input, output and cache tokens with a cost at the rates the
operator declared, and files the total once with the model, thinking level,
harness version and brief language, under the name the session was created
with. A run with any unmetered turn files nothing, so the record carries the
whole meter or none. Effort is shown beside a run and never ranks it.

A seeded random policy plays the same budget through the same control
surface, drawing from thirteen keys the brief teaches, weighted towards
movement and confirmation, and never reading the screen, following the
convention that anchors a score on random and human play
\citep{mnih2015humanlevel,hafner2021benchmarking,arc2026arcagi3}. The rungs
it reaches are what the environment gives away, and they are the floor of the
ladder.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:results}

The field at submission is \NmodelsDistinct{} models from \Nvendors{}
vendors at the \NbudgetMin{}-minute budget, and a seeded random baseline. A
run is listed under the name declared when its session was created, and a
model is reported by one session: \NrepeatModels{} models were run more than
once, and each is reported by the longest session it played, the
\NrepeatSessions{} others staying in the snapshot, \NrepeatNever{} of them a
session that never sent a key.\ifnum\Naliased>0{} \Naliased{} runs were created under
names that were not the model, and are listed under the model the operator
identified, \aliaslist, with the declared names kept in the snapshot.\fi{}
No run in this field carries a usage report, so
tokens and thinking level are not reported, and \Shelp{} of the \Nsessions{}
sessions fetched the brief from the session, in English, the rest reading the
published page. Every session is public on the leaderboard with its video,
replay timeline and metric record, and the catalogue snapshot the paper is
generated from is committed beside the paper source. \Cref{app:field} lists
the runs by name. This section reads the field for what it says about the
instrument: how much of the ladder is open, how far it separates models, and
how a budget is spent.

\tbd{The present field reports one run per model. A sweep of repeated
runs per model under the fixed harness, and a human
reference run under the same budget and interface reported to the
human-baseline checklist \citep{wei2025humanbaselines}, are the two
additions the field most needs.}

\subsection{Headroom}

Every rung is measured, and the compass is the highest the field reaches at
this budget, by one session. \Sitem{} of \Nsessions{} sessions pick something
up, and \Smap{} reach the world map, every one of them credited by the save
the game wrote; \Sstayed{} spend the whole budget in the scene they started
in. One session, \ScompassLabel, holds the compass, which the world offers in
one place, the cabinet beside the hermit, so it is the first session to clear
a rung beyond the world map; it does not recruit the companion whose house
that visit opens. No session recruits a companion, gains experience or holds
a book: all \Sread{} character records stand at level \Slevel{} with
\SexpSum{} experience and \Sskills{} skill, the state the session booted
into, so every rung from the companion on is open. \Cref{fig:ladder} shows the
ladder by model. Comparable instruments place their fields at a
similar point: BALROG reports its best model at $1.5\%$ progression on
NetHack \citep{paglieri2025balrog} and VideoGameBench reports the best
vision-language models completing $0.48\%$ of its suite
\citep{zhang2025videogamebench}. The distance from the compass to the
fourteen books is the headroom the benchmark holds.

\subsection{Discrimination}

Three rungs divide the field, and they divide it differently. The chest and
the exit are both in the first room, and the sessions that searched one are
not all the sessions that found the other: \SleftNoItem{} crossed without the
chest, \SitemNoLeft{} searched it without crossing, and \Sboth{} did both.
The count of rungs is therefore a summary of what a run achieved and not a
position on one path, which is why every rung reached is credited whatever
the order. The random policy reaches \QrandomRungs{} of the eight rungs in
\QrandomBudgetMin{} minutes: it acts, and in a small room a random walk
searches the chest within the hour. It never writes a save, so the world map
is the first rung a random walk does not reach, and the chest rung separates
models from one another but not from the floor. The compass divides the
field a third time, one session from the rest, and above it the field is
undivided: no session recruits the companion, which is where the ladder will
next separate models. The world-map rung also shows why progress is read from
the machine. The session record keeps, beside the save, a screen fingerprint
of the world map that the harness latches for its replay; the two agree on
\SmapScreen{} crossings, and \SmapSaveOnly{} crossing that the fingerprint
never latched is credited by the save alone, while no fingerprint latched
without a save behind it.

\subsection{Use of the Budget}

The hour is enough for the first room and, once in the field, for the
compass. \Smap{} sessions reached the world map and one reached the cabinet a
walk south of the exit; every session ran to its budget, and none was cut
short. The crossing is timed by the game: the first save it writes on the
world map trails the crossing by at most the two-minute cadence of the
attempts, and by that clock the \SsaveN{} crossings fall between
\SsaveFirst{} and \SsaveLast{} minutes into the session, median
\SsaveMedian{}. The session that went on to the compass crossed at
\ScompassSaveMin{} minutes, after \ScompassExitActs{} actions, and spent
the rest of the hour beyond the exit without recruiting the companion. The
protocol supports budgets to twenty-four hours, the setting
in which the later rungs and a completion time come within reach, and
deliberation is priced in wall clock, an efficiency framing shared with
ARC-AGI-3 and TextQuests \citep{arc2026arcagi3,phan2025textquests}.

\tbd{Long runs that exercise the additive long-horizon report are the
measurement that separates a slow model from a stuck one; the protocol and the
report already support them, and the field does not yet contain them.}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented \bench{}, a long-horizon benchmark that runs an unmodified 1996
open-world role-playing game, gives every model the same brief and control
surface, and scores it in the terms the game keeps about itself. The environment is adversarial in ways that are named and
checkable: an out-of-distribution $320\times200$ isometric frame,
Traditional Chinese as the only channel through which objectives are stated,
and a world that supplies no score. Progress read from the memory of the environment and from a save the
game performs for itself cannot be manufactured by visual churn, and every
rung of the ladder is an event the model has to cause. The ladder ends at the
ending of the game: recovering the fourteen books is a state the archive
records, so a model that finishes leaves proof of it, and the benchmark then
ranks by the time it took. Both scales exist from the start, so the first
finishing model changes what the leaderboard measures, and the run that gets
there is on record with its video and every key it pressed.

\section{The Brief and the Control Surface}
\label{app:brief}
A reference-harness run receives the tool adapter note and the text the game
server serves at \texttt{/api/help}: the calls that constitute play, the
rules of a run covering budget, recording and publication, the
isometric key mapping with an explicit warning that arrow-key intuitions do
not hold, the opening route as far as the compass, and a manual of the
controls, menus, combat and attributes of the game. The page at
\texttt{agents.md} is generated from the same source for clients that create
their own sessions, and adds session creation and the note that any starting
name is acceptable. The English and Chinese versions are equivalent in rules,

serves the Chinese brief by default, and the record of a run carries the

rather than to a session: after a run, the harness files the usage meter of
\cref{sec:metrics} under the name the session was created with, and the
broker merges it into the catalogue entry of that run.

\section{Rung Definitions and the Save Procedure}
\label{app:metrics}
A key is held for a requested 5 to 1200 emulated frames, 10 by default, with
six frames between the keys of a list, and an action returns once the screen
has reacted and held still, waiting up to 300 frames for a black screen to be
redrawn when a scene changes; nothing the server observes about the screen
enters any score. The recording journal
logs every key the server presses on the core with its hold, and the seeded
sequence of a random run is recomputed offline and compared with that
journal: for the random runs reported here the two agree key for key, and on
the local runner single ten-frame taps of every call shape moved the
character one tile per tap in the position the game saved. The save attempt reads the height of the menu the
game draws, which grows by one 20-pixel row per entry: six rows on the world
map and four inside a scene, and only the six-row menu carries the system
entry through which the game saves. An attempt opens the menu and reads its
height, withdrawing on the four rows of a scene and saving only where the six
of the world map appear, so what admits a save is the game's own menu and not
a reading of where the party stands; and a look the model takes waits on the
same single-player lock the attempt holds, so the model never observes the
screen mid-save.
Attempts wait for a two-second gap
between the keys the model sends, recur every two minutes, and are made once more
shortly before the budget lapses, into a save slot reserved for the
benchmark, and the time of the first save is kept as the time of the crossing.
The item rung compares the live bag with the bag at the first read
of the run; the compass rung looks for item \CompassId{} in the bag, whose
name in the shipped item table is \CompassName{}, and the book rung for items
\BookFirst{}--\BookLast{} in the bag or the four carried slots of the
protagonist, each read from the live machine and corroborated by the save the
game writes, and kept once seen; and the shipped names of all fifteen items
are checked by the test suite.

\section{Session and Infrastructure Parameters}
\label{app:infra}
The default budget is \DefaultBudgetSec{}\,s, raised from 1200\,s in
September 2026, and is configurable to 24\,h; there is no inactivity
teardown; the concurrency cap is 24 sessions per 8-vCPU host, set
from a measurement of 32 sessions holding native 70.09\,fps on 8\,GiB with
memory binding before CPU, at about 123\,MB of game copy per run, and the
service runs on 16\,GiB. Emulation is DOSBox Pure
through libretro at a fixed 26800 cycles, the speed of a 486DX2-66 and
period-correct for the game, with no sound card, so the observation is visual
only, and with per-session private copies of the game and save directories.
Recordings are stored as $16\times10$-tile zlib deltas; published video is
the native $320\times200$ frame above a 32-pixel strip naming the actor, the
action number, the keys held and the clock, played at \DefaultReplaySpeed{}
times speed, or faster for long runs, with a timeline sidecar of a few
kilobytes carrying
every keypress and its hold duration for the scrubbable replay. A
character-state read costs about 1.2\,ms through serialisation into a reused
buffer and is taken after every action, with gains latched against the
opening bag, so an item picked up and later consumed still credits its rung.
Outside scored runs the server can checkpoint and resume a session
and serve many visitors from private workers behind one gateway; a scored run
has neither. The leaderboard is a static page polling published JSON; only
the live viewers of a running session, capped at twelve, reach its worker.

\section{The Fourteen Books}
\label{app:books}
The ending requires the \BookCount{} books, which the game stores as item
records \BookFirst{} to \BookLast{} of its item table, named after the
novels: \booklist. A book counts as held when it is in the shared bag or in
the four carried slots of the protagonist, which is how the final rung of
the ladder is read.
With all fourteen in hand and sufficient reputation, the game opens its final
sequence at a shrine on the far side of the map, where the books are placed
and a last battle decides the ending \citep{bahamut2023walkthrough}.
